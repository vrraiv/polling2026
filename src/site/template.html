How the model works

  1. National environment. Polls-only: the mean of the generic-ballot aggregators, converted to a two-party margin. Polls + fundamentals: that number blended, by inverse variance, with an estimated fundamentals model (Gallup approval, the previous House result, real gas-price change; inflation, income and unemployment tested). The polls get about 55% of the weight (about 70% in the bias-adjusted model, whose polling SD is smaller once the bias is removed). The polling bias shifts only the polls, not the fundamentals.
  2. Race priors. Each race starts at 2×PVI + national environment + incumbency (+2.5 points for House incumbents, +2 for Senate). PVI is Cook-style, on the 2026 district lines, so it already reflects mid-decade redistricting in TX, CA, MO, NC, UT, OH, VA, FL, TN, LA and AL.
  3. Poll averages. Recency-weighted (21-day half-life), scaled by √sample size, likely voters preferred over registered voters and adults. Pollsters with many polls are down-weighted, and partisan-sponsored polls are shifted against their sponsor (2 pts for Senate, 4 pts for House district polls).
  4. Blend. Poll average and prior are combined by precision (normal–normal). The poll-average error is set from history (race-level SD 7.4 pts in midterms, two-party); the Senate prior's error is assumed at 10 pts and the House district error at 6 pts.
  5. Simulation. 40,000 draws. Each draw shares a national polling error across every race and both chambers, plus a Census-region shock and a random slope on state lean (a polarisation miss), plus race-specific errors. Each race's total variance is held fixed. All national and race errors are fat-tailed (t-distribution, 5 df). Details in the technical appendix.
  6. Control. House: 218 seats; same-party and uncontested seats are fixed; CA-6 (Kevin Kiley, independent) is counted for neither party. Senate: Democrats need 51 seats (VP Vance breaks ties); 34 Dem-caucus and 31 Republican seats are not up. Independents (Osborn, NE; Achilles, ID) are reported separately.

Judgment calls & caveats

  • Fundamentals are estimated on 1962–2022, and 2026 is outside that range on two inputs. Approval (Gallup-equivalent about 35.6%) is below every past midterm, and the 29% real rise in gas prices is above the largest in the data. Both push toward Democrats, so the fundamentals estimate leans on extrapolation.
  • Polling bias. See the Polling bias section: the bias-adjusted model shifts every race by the expected miss; the unadjusted model takes the polls at face value.
  • Michigan. Haley Stevens endorsed Mike Rogers on Nov 1, after most polls were taken. This isn't in the model.
  • Alaska uses ranked-choice voting and has several "Dan Sullivan" candidates on the ballot. It's modeled as a head-to-head. Montana is a genuine three-way race, modeled from polls only (best non-Republican vs. Republican).
  • District polls are sparse, and many are sponsored by parties or PACs (48 of 132 since July are Democratic-sponsored). They are shrunk toward the prior.

Technical appendix

A. Fundamentals model (estimated)

Outcome: the two-party House vote margin of the president's party (1962–2022, Wikipedia infoboxes). Predictors are measured before the election: Gallup approval (polls ending July 15–October 31; American Presidency Project), the president's party's House margin two years earlier, CPI inflation (September over September, unrevised CPI-U), the real change in gasoline prices (gasoline CPI ÷ CPI-U, September over September), real disposable income per capita growth, and the 12-month change in unemployment. Every subset of up to four predictors is fit by OLS and scored by leave-one-election-out RMSE. The 2026 estimate averages the top five specifications, weighted by 1/RMSE².

B. Error model

For race r in simulation s: margin = μr + σnat·zs + uregion(r),s + bs·leanr + εr,s. The national shock z is t(5), shared by every House and Senate race. Region shocks u (four Census regions) and the lean slope b are normal. The race term ε is t(5) with variance sdrace² − σregion² − σb²·lean² (floored at a quarter of the total), so each race's marginal probability matches its calibrated error and only the joint distribution changes.

C. Estimators at a glance

  • Poll weight = ½age/half-life × √(min(n, 3000)/600) × population weight (LV 1.0, RV 0.8, adults 0.5) ÷ √(polls by that pollster). Half-life: 21 days for races, 14 for the generic ballot. Two-party margins throughout.
  • Poll-average SD (Senate) = √(7.4² + max(spread, 4)²/neff), with neff = (Σw)²/Σw². Blend: μ = (μpoll/σpoll² + μprior/σprior²) / (1/σpoll² + 1/σprior²).
  • National SDs: RMSE of the final generic-ballot miss, two-party, 1998–2022 (unadjusted models); the predictive SD of the decay-weighted bias estimate (bias-adjusted models). Senate national SD is the Senate per-cycle miss RMSE, scaled the same way.
  • Calibration data: 538's raw_polls.csv (final 21 days, 1998–2022), averaged within race before comparing to results.
  • Monte Carlo error: with 40,000 draws, a probability near 50% has a standard error of about 0.25 points.

Polling bias

How far the final generic-ballot polling average has missed the national House vote, on a two-party basis, 1998–2022. Positive means Democrats beat their polls.

Re-estimating the bias for 2026

A flat average treats 1998 and 2022 alike. The re-estimate weights elections by recency (half-lives of 6 to 16 years), weights midterms 2–3× presidential years, and tests covariates: party in the White House, which party held the House, a second-term flag, and a president-specific effect shrunk toward the mean. Specifications are compared by leave-one-election-out prediction error, weighted toward recent midterms.
