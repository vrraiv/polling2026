How each version scored

Brier score: the average squared gap between the forecast probability and the result (1 if the Democratic side won, 0 if not); 0 is perfect and 50% everywhere scores 0.25. Log loss punishes confident misses harder. "Close races" are fixed from the frozen forecast before any result: every race any published version gave the Democratic side between 2% and 98%. Margin error is the actual two-party margin minus the forecast's expected margin (positive = Democrats did better).

    How it works

    1. National environment. The mean of five generic-ballot polling averages (two-party), blended with an estimated fundamentals model: Gallup approval, the previous House result and the real change in gas prices, with inflation, income and unemployment tested and found weak.
    2. Race priors. Senate races start at 2×PVI + national environment + incumbency (+2 pts, assumed rather than estimated). House districts use a model estimated on 2006–2024 district results: . PVI is on the 2026 district lines, so it reflects the mid-decade redistricting in 11 states.
    3. Poll averages. Prolific pollsters are down-weighted and larger samples count more. All of these settings were estimated from 538's poll archive.
    4. Blend. Poll average and prior are combined by precision.
    5. Simulation. 40,000 draws with a shared national polling error, Census-region shocks, a random slope on state lean, and race-specific errors, all fat-tailed. Control: 218 House seats; 51 Senate seats (VP Vance breaks ties).
    6. Three headline versions. Polls only uses the generic-ballot averages for the national environment. Polls + fundamentals blends that with the estimated fundamentals model and takes the polls as they are, treating the historical bias as symmetric noise. Bias-adjusted applies only the corrections that reduce error in a real-time test (estimated from earlier elections, applied to the next): the generic-ballot polls (not the fundamentals) move by pts, treated as a mean shift with its own uncertainty, and each Senate poll average gets a state-lean term (results since 2010 have landed further toward state partisanship than polls, 0.08 pts per point of lean). Senate polls get no across-the-board shift (): that correction did not pass the test. See Polling bias.

    Judgment calls and caveats
    • Fundamentals extrapolate. 2026 approval (Gallup-equivalent ~35.6%) is below every past midterm, and the 29% real rise in gas prices is above anything in the 1962–2022 data.
    • Senate incumbency (+2 pts for a sitting senator running) is assumed, not estimated; it only enters the prior, which gets about of the weight in polled races.
    • House model. Estimated on 2006–2024 district results (appendix D). In recent cycles it has undercounted Democratic wins in contested seats by about 6 on average (10 in 2018), so the House seat estimate may be slightly conservative for Democrats.
    • Idaho has a single poll, from August; with few late polls its forecast leans on that poll more than its age warrants.
    • Michigan. Haley Stevens endorsed Mike Rogers on Nov 1, after most polls were taken. Not in the model.
    • Alaska uses ranked-choice voting and has several "Dan Sullivan" candidates on the ballot; modeled as a head-to-head. Montana is a three-way race, modeled from polls only.
    • Independents (Osborn NE, Achilles ID, Bengs SD) are counted separately from either party. CA-6 (Kevin Kiley, independent) is counted for neither party.
    • District polls are sparse and often party-sponsored; they're shrunk toward the prior.

    How the Bias-adjusted model is built

    Re-estimating the bias for 2026 (decay weights and covariates)

    Elections are weighted by recency (half-lives of 6–16 years), midterms 2–3× presidential years, and covariates are tested: party in the White House, House majority, second term, and a president-specific effect. Specifications are compared by leave-one-election-out error.

    Technical appendix

    A. Fundamentals model (estimated)

    Outcome: the two-party House vote margin of the president's party, 1962–2022. Predictors are measured before the election: Gallup approval (polls ending July 15–October 31), the president's party's House margin two years earlier, CPI inflation and the real change in gasoline prices (September over September), real disposable income per capita growth, and the change and level of unemployment. Every subset of up to four predictors is fit by OLS and scored by leave-one-election-out RMSE; the 2026 estimate averages the top five, weighted by 1/RMSE².

    B. Error model

    For race r in simulation s: margin = μr + σnat·zs + uregion(r),s + bs·leanr + εr,s. The national shock z is t(), shared by every House and Senate race; region shocks u (four Census regions) and the lean slope b are normal; ε is t() with variance sdrace² − σregion² − σb²·lean² (floored at a quarter of the total), so each race's own probability stays calibrated and only the joint distribution changes.

    F. Estimators at a glance

    • Poll weight = ½age/half-life × √(min(n, 3000)/600) × population weight ÷ √(polls by that pollster); settings in section E. Two-party margins throughout.
    • Poll-average SD (Senate) = √(² + ²/n), n = polls in the last three weeks (older polls count half). Fitted on 705 final-three-week averages, 1998–2022: . Blend: μ = (μpoll/σpoll² + μprior/σprior²)/(1/σpoll² + 1/σprior²), σprior = in polled Senate races (set so polls get the historically optimal weight; see C), for unpolled Senate races (robust error of the prior alone), for House districts (estimated; see D).
    • National SDs: RMSE of the final generic-ballot miss (polls only; polls + fundamentals); predictive SD of the decay-weighted bias estimate (adjusted). The Senate national SD is the Senate per-cycle miss RMSE, scaled the same way.
    • Calibration data: 538's raw_polls.csv (final 21 days, 1998–2022), averaged within race before comparison with results.
    • Monte Carlo error: 40,000 draws, so a probability near 50% has a standard error of about 0.25 points.
